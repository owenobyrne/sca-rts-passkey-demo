Passkeys in a payments portal

What replacing SMS codes actually involves for the three things a customer authorises: signing in, adding a beneficiary, and moving money.

    Demonstration only. The “portal server” runs in this tab, so nothing here is trustworthy as an authorisation decision — it exists to show the protocol, the checks and the failure modes. No money moves, no data leaves your browser, and your passkey stays on your device.

    What this does not protect against

    The passkey prompt shows the site, not the transaction. “Verify your identity for this portal” is all the operating system says — WebAuthn’s trusted user interface answers who are you authenticating to, never what are you approving. The amount and payee come from the page, which is precisely the component you might not trust.

    So dynamic linking defeats tampering that happens after the action reaches the server’s authoritative record: a modified request body, a tampered API hop, a replayed assertion, a mix-up between what was authorised and what gets executed. Those are the attacks the buttons in step 5 demonstrate, and they are real.

    It does not defeat a compromised page. Follow it through: hostile script initiates €15,000 to its own IBAN; the server stores that as authoritative and mints a challenge committing to it; the page displays “€150 to Merchant XYZ”; the customer approves a prompt that mentions neither; the assertion commits to €15,000; the server re-hashes, every check goes green, and the payment executes. Dynamic linking binds the code to what the server was told, not to what the human saw. Those are the same thing only while the page is honest.

    Two things narrow that gap. Secure Payment Confirmation moves the display into the browser, so display and signature share one enforced data path — it defends against hostile JavaScript, though not against a compromised browser or operating system, and it is Chromium-only. A second device with its own display — a mobile app that renders the amount and payee and takes the biometric there — is the only real answer, because compromising the laptop no longer yields the approval.

    Worth stating plainly for anyone migrating away from SMS: if the text message contains the amount and payee, moving to passkeys loses an independent display channel while gaining phishing resistance. That is an argument for passkeys everywhere plus a second-channel confirmation above a value threshold — not for keeping SMS.

    The controls that actually close this gap do not depend on display integrity at all: dual authorisation with two people on two devices, beneficiary cooling-off periods, out-of-band verification for new payees, and value limits.

    The passkey on a phone, the portal on a desktop

    This works, and it is a first-class flow: hybrid transport, also called cross-device authentication. The desktop shows a QR code, the phone scans it, the two prove physical proximity over Bluetooth, and the assertion travels back through an encrypted tunnel. Every current desktop browser supports it, with iOS and Android acting as the authenticator.

    The Bluetooth proximity step is not decoration. It is what stops an attacker relaying a QR code to a distant victim: the phone will only complete the exchange with a device that is physically beside it, so a QR shown on a phishing page cannot be redeemed from somewhere else.

    But a passkey on a phone is not a second channel. The phone’s prompt names the site, exactly as a local prompt would — it does not show the amount or the payee. Reaching for your phone feels like out-of-band transaction confirmation, and it is not: hybrid moves where the key lives, not what the customer gets to verify. Closing the gap described above still needs an application that renders the transaction on the phone and takes the approval there.

    To try it here: on the desktop, press Use a phone or security key in step 1, scan the QR code the browser shows, and approve on the phone — the passkey is created there, in iCloud Keychain or Google Password Manager. Every later step in the demo will then offer it. That button asks for authenticatorAttachment: "cross-platform", which is the whole of the site’s influence: whether your phone is remembered for next time is negotiated between the phone and the browser, and no site can request it, observe it, or rely on it. Chrome with an Android phone generally offers to remember; a QR-based iPhone pairing generally does not.

    Two things to check if the QR never appears: Bluetooth must be on at both ends, and a passkey is scoped to one relying party — one enrolled against localhost will not work on the hosted copy, or the other way round.

    Expect friction. Unless the desktop and phone are linked, or the passkey already syncs to the desktop through the same platform account, it is QR plus camera plus Bluetooth on every authentication — noticeably slower than typing an SMS code, and paid on every payment. The usual answer is to enrol a passkey on the desktop as well and keep the phone for bootstrapping and recovery.

    Two deployment blockers worth checking before committing: corporate builds that disable Bluetooth or the camera cannot do hybrid at all, and virtual desktop environments often cannot reach a local authenticator without specific WebAuthn redirection. Finance teams are disproportionately likely to be working in exactly those environments.

    Your fallback is your real security level

    A phishing-resistant passkey with an SMS reset path is a phishable system: the attacker simply forces the downgrade. This is the most common way a passkey rollout fails to deliver the security it promised, and no amount of WebAuthn correctness fixes it.

    The workable pattern is an unequal fallback rather than an equivalent one. A session authenticated by the fallback can view and prepare, but cannot add a beneficiary or release a payment above a threshold without either a passkey or an operator-verified re-enrolment. Enrol two authenticators at onboarding — a second device, or a security key — so that losing one is routine rather than an incident.

    The same reasoning applies to what an attacker gains from each action. Beneficiary creation deserves the strongest step-up you have, because it converts into every future payment.

    The three corrections

    1. userVerification: "discouraged" defeats SCA. PSD2 Article 4(30) requires two independent elements. A passkey assertion with only UP set evidences possession alone. The second element — inherence (biometric) or knowledge (device PIN) — is evidenced only by the UV flag, and you get that by asking for userVerification: "required" and then verifying the flag server-side. Asking politely is not enough; the flag is the evidence.

    2. There is no extensions.payment dictionary on navigator.credentials.get(). The real mechanism is Secure Payment Confirmation: a PaymentRequest with the secure-payment-confirmation method, whose response.details is a PublicKeyCredential. Its original purpose is letting a merchant origin invoke a bank’s credential cross-origin, which a first-party portal does not need. But it has a second property that a first-party portal very much does want: the browser, not the page, renders the amount and payee and signs those same values, so a compromised page cannot display one figure and sign another. This demo therefore treats SPC as a step-up — offered for payments where the browser supports it, with plain WebAuthn and a page-rendered panel as the fallback. This demo requests it only where the browser exposes PaymentRequest, because the SPC specification has create() throw NotSupportedError where SPC is unsupported, which would block enrolment outright.

    3. Hashing the transaction is only half of dynamic linking. A hash is one-way, so a challenge alone tells the server nothing on its return journey. The server must keep the authoritative record (and the nonce) alongside the challenge, then re-hash what it is about to do and require that it equals the challenge inside the signed clientDataJSON. Without that final comparison, an attacker changes the amount — or the IBAN — after authorisation and the signature still verifies. Try it in step 5.

    Article 5 — what “dynamically linked” actually demands

    RTS Article 5(1) requires that:

    • (a) the payer is made aware of the amount and the payee;
    • (b) the authentication code generated is specific to the amount and the payee agreed to;
    • (c) the code changes if the amount or the payee changes;
    • (d) any change to the amount or payee invalidates the code.

    In WebAuthn terms: (a) is the confirmation panel in step 4; (b) and (c) come from committing the transaction into the challenge; (d) comes from the server refusing to execute anything that does not re-hash to the signed challenge, plus single-use consumption of the challenge.

    Article 5(2) adds confidentiality, authenticity and integrity requirements for the amount and payee “throughout all phases of the authentication”, which is why the origin check and the crossOrigin check are not optional extras.

    Where passkeys get interesting under the RTS

    Synced passkeys and “possession”. A passkey with BE=1, BS=1 is backed up to the customer’s platform account and present on several devices. The mainstream industry position is that this still satisfies possession, but supervisory comfort varies, and corporate IT departments may object to keys syncing into personal cloud accounts. Record the flags on every assertion — this demo does — so the analysis is evidenced rather than asserted, and consider requiring device-bound credentials or enterprise attestation for your highest-value approvers.

    Independence (Art. 9). The elements must be independent: breach of one must not compromise the other. On a passkey, possession is a key held in the authenticator’s secure hardware and inherence is verified locally by the platform — but the analysis rests on the device’s secure execution environment, not on WebAuthn alone.

    Corporate access is the hard part. Multiple named users per client, four-eyes approval, delegated authority and staff turnover. Passkeys are per-person-per-device, so offboarding must revoke credentials and re-enrolment needs an out-of-band identity check you are willing to defend. Recovery, not cryptography, is where this work actually hurts.

    Attestation. This demo requests attestation: "none". A PSP that wants assurance about which authenticator model holds the key needs attestation and a metadata service to check it against, weighed against privacy and enrolment friction.

    Relying-party identifier. Hosted on GitHub Pages the RP ID is the *.github.io subdomain, which works because github.io sits on the Public Suffix List. A real portal scopes credentials to its own registrable domain and lives with the fact that RP ID cannot be changed later without re-enrolling every customer.

    What a real deployment needs that this does not have
    • A genuine server: challenge state, credential registry, beneficiary records, ledger and every check in step 6 must run somewhere the customer cannot reach. Here they run in your tab.
    • Article 2 transaction monitoring, Article 3 auditing, and Article 19 fraud-rate calculation and reporting — the TRA exemption is earned by an audited fraud rate, not by a dropdown.
    • A real Verification of Payee call to the payee’s PSP, rather than a picker.
    • Passkey lifecycle and account recovery, multi-user corporate accounts, and a restricted fallback for customers without a usable authenticator.
